The case for listening to what you read

A short essay · 6 min read

Most of what we read on a screen is read in fragments: a paragraph between meetings, a page on the train, half an article before the tab is lost among thirty others. Listening changes that. A long essay becomes something you can finish while cooking, walking or resting your eyes.

Hearing a text also slows it down in a useful way. A voice cannot skim. It reads every sentence at the same patient pace, and arguments that were easy to skip over on the page suddenly have to be followed from beginning to end.

Reading with your ears and eyes

The best of both comes from doing the two together. When the words light up as they are spoken, your eyes stay anchored to the line, and it becomes much harder to drift. Many readers with dyslexia or attention difficulties describe this as the first time a long text felt manageable.

It helps with languages, too. Following a voice through an unfamiliar technical text teaches you how its words are meant to sound, and the rhythm of a well-made sentence becomes easier to hear.

Why it should stay on your computer

What you read says a great deal about you. A read-aloud tool that sends every page to a server learns your work, your health questions and your private interests. Voices that run on your own computer avoid that entirely, and they keep working on a plane or in a cabin without signal.
